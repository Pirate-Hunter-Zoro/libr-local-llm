\begin{frame}{The whole thing in one sentence}

\keybox{\large A local coding assistant that is \textbf{always there}, \textbf{gives cluster
resources back} the moment somebody else needs them, and \textbf{restarts itself} when it dies.}

\vspace{0.8em}
Three problems it solves, all of which you have today:

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.34\textwidth}p{0.30\textwidth}p{0.30\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Today} & \textbf{Why it hurts} & \textbf{After} \\
\midrule
You run \texttt{ollama-up} by hand & nothing runs until you notice & it is already up \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.8em}
\textbf{And one problem it does not solve:} it is not Claude. Slide 13 is that conversation,
and it is worth reading before the rest.

\vspace{0.6em}
\textbf{Step 4 is the one that is easy to skip and ruins everything.} Without it: the supervisor
releases a node, notices one second later that it is below target, and re-submits --- winning the
race against the very job it was trying to help. The fleet then looks maximally rude while running
code written to be polite.

\vspace{0.5em}
\keybox{The rule is not a timer. It is: \textbf{do not ask for that resource again until the job
that triggered the yield is no longer pending} --- with a 15-minute floor underneath it.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which thing gets given up first}

Ordering principle: \emph{surrender the unit holding the most, that costs the least to bring back.}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{clcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Rank} & \textbf{Unit} & \textbf{Holds} & \textbf{Costs to restore} \\
\midrule
1 & an idle vLLM replica & 1 GPU & a few minutes \\
2 & an idle ollama server & 1 GPU & \meas\ 4m46s for 65\,GB \\
3 & a compute306 booking & \textbf{4 GPUs} & minutes; biggest single win \\
4 & a \emph{busy} vLLM replica & 1 GPU & minutes + the in-flight work \\
5 & the colibr\`i backend & 1 GPU + $\sim$500\,GB RAM & \proj\ $\sim$31 minutes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.8em}
Notice what the ordering does to colibr\`i without anybody writing a special case for it: it holds
the \emph{least} GPU and takes \emph{longest} to come back, so it lands at the bottom naturally.

\vspace{0.5em}
{\scriptsize \textbf{Why restore cost dominates.} There is no local scratch disk on these nodes,
so every cold model load is an NFS read at \meas\ $\sim$230\,MB/s. A fleet that thrashes is worse
than a fleet that is simply smaller. Hysteresis is not tuning --- it is correctness.}

\end{frame}

\vspace{0.2em}
{\small \textbf{Each generation submits its successor the moment it starts}, with a dependency that
fires on \emph{any} termination --- walltime, a crash, a node vanishing. A pending job costs
nothing, so the queued successor is free insurance.}

\vspace{0.3em}
\keybox{\small \textbf{The kill switch is a file, not a signal.} \texttt{fleet down} writes
\texttt{STOP} \emph{before} it cancels anything, so the successor starts, reads it, and exits in
two seconds. It works when the supervisor is wedged and when its node is gone --- a kill switch
that depends on the thing it kills is not one.}

%----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What this is not}

\keybox{\large It is not Claude, and no amount of plumbing makes it Claude.}

\vspace{0.7em}
The gap is the \textbf{weights}, not the serving. Three specific things stay true after everything
above is built:

\begin{itemize}\small
\item \textbf{The ceiling is \texttt{gpt-oss:120b}}, a good open 120B coder --- and it needs the
      cluster's only four-GPU node, which is exactly the resource the design says never to camp on.
\item \textbf{The 744B model is not automatically better.} Its int4 container measures
      \textbf{$-$8.2 percentage points} of quantization damage, concentrated on the hardest
      questions. A 744B model at int4 beating a well-served 120B at 8-bit is a \emph{hypothesis},
      and nobody has run the comparison on our tasks.
\item \textbf{No routing layer fixes this.} No arrangement of three engines is smarter than the
      best model among them. The fleet buys coverage, uptime and politeness --- not intelligence.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.6em}
\textbf{So why build it?} One reason, and it is enough:

\keybox{A frontier-scale model that \textbf{may read PHI}. No hosted service will ever be allowed
to do that here. That is the capability, and it is not available any other way.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What it costs us}

The hardware is bought and powered. The real bill is in two other currencies.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Currency} & \textbf{Amount} & \textbf{Who pays} \\
\midrule
other people's queue time & bounded by caps + yielding & colleagues \\
our own fair-share & $\sim$500{,}000 usage units at steady state & \textbf{our own research jobs} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.5em}
{\small \textbf{The second one is the surprise.} A supervisor running around the clock holds
Slurm's minimum billable unit --- two CPUs, always, because cores here carry two threads and
nothing smaller can be allocated. At the cluster's two-day decay that reaches a steady-state usage
contribution \textbf{roughly the size of everything this account currently runs}. Fair-share is
the heaviest term in the priority formula, so a fleet that never sleeps makes the PSYCH-ASR and
TRD-EHR jobs it exists to serve schedule \emph{worse}.}

\vspace{0.4em}
\keybox{\small \textbf{The fix is boring and it works:} when the fleet has held no GPUs for two hours, the
supervisor exits too. Typing \texttt{fleet code} brings it back in about thirty seconds. A
supervisor watching an empty fleet is indistinguishable, from your seat, from no supervisor at
all.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three cluster facts nobody had checked}

Found while planning this, on the live scheduler. Each one changes something.

\begin{enumerate}\small
\item \textbf{The \texttt{c3} partition can silently freeze our server.} \texttt{c3\_short}
      outranks \texttt{c3} on the same six nodes, and the configured response is
      \emph{suspend} --- \texttt{SIGSTOP}, not requeue. A suspended server keeps its VRAM (so the
      preemption helps nobody) and stops mid-generation with the socket open, so the client gets
      no error, just silence.
      \textbf{Consequence:} the fleet never uses \texttt{c3}. The README currently recommends it
      for long-lived servers; that advice is a trap.

\item \textbf{Asking for memory silently buys CPUs.} Memory is capped at 12\,GB per CPU here, so
      \texttt{-{}-mem=64G -{}-cpus-per-task=4} does not get 4 CPUs --- it gets 6. Our careful
      CPU-rightsizing argument was being undone by the memory line the whole time.

\item \textbf{Almost nothing on this cluster is actually blocked.} Of the 27 pending jobs visible
      the day this was written, \textbf{all 27} were waiting on a start time they had asked for,
      not on resources. A naive ``somebody is waiting, give a node back'' rule would surrender the
      entire fleet, continuously, to jobs it was not blocking.
      \textbf{Consequence:} the yield filter is the load-bearing part, and the yield path has to be
      tested on purpose because it will rarely fire on its own.
\end{enumerate}

\end{frame}
