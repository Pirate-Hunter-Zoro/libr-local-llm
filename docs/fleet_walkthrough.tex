\begin{frame}{Why a 744B model runs on a machine that cannot hold it}

A 744B mixture-of-experts activates only about \textbf{40B parameters per token} --- and only the
\emph{routed experts} change from one token to the next.

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tikzpicture}[
  font=\scriptsize, >={Stealth[length=2mm]},
  tier/.style={rounded corners=3pt, align=center, inner sep=5pt, minimum height=13mm},
  lbl/.style={font=\tiny, inner sep=1.5pt}
]
\node[tier, draw=green!45!black, thick, fill=green!14, minimum width=32mm] (v) at (0,0)
  {\textbf{VRAM} --- A40, 46\,GB\\[2pt]dense + attention + KV\\$\approx$23\,GB};
\node[tier, draw=blue!55!black, thick, fill=blue!12, minimum width=42mm] (r) at (6.6,0)
  {\textbf{RAM} --- 1\,TB on every node\\[2pt]\textbf{all 19,456 routed experts}\\372\,GB, fully resident};
\node[tier, draw=black!45, fill=black!6, minimum width=32mm] (d) at (13.4,0)
  {\textbf{disk} --- the studies share\\[2pt]cold start only\\\textbf{0\,MB/s during decode}};
\draw[->, thick, black!60] (d) -- node[lbl, above] {read once, at startup} (r);
\draw[<->, very thick, blue!55!black] (r) -- node[lbl, above] {12.1\,GB per token} (v);
\end{tikzpicture}}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.3em}
\textbf{Think of it as a JIT, but for weights.} A compiler JIT never compiles the whole program --- it
watches what actually runs. colibr\`i makes the same bet: parameters are not state to be held, they
are \textbf{data to be staged}, exactly when the router proves they are needed.

\vspace{0.4em}
{\scriptsize \textbf{Placement decides speed and nothing else.} The router's decisions and the
weights' precision are identical whether an expert answered from VRAM or from disk.}

\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Where the time actually goes: two halves, roughly equal}

Measured by colibr\`i's own profiler on a \textbf{2-socket Ice Lake, 48 cores, model fully
resident} --- our CPU class:

\keybox{\parbox{0.72\textwidth}{\ttfamily\small
4.68 tok/s\hfill = 214 ms per token \\[0.3em]
\hspace*{1em}expert read: 12.1 GB / 131.9 GB/s\hfill = \ \ 92 ms\quad(43\%) \\
\hspace*{1em}attention, dense, router, orchestration\hfill = 122 ms\quad(57\%)}}

\vspace{0.7em}
An independent profile on different hardware agrees: expert-matmul 46\%, attention 26\%.

\vspace{0.6em}
\keybox{\textbf{This is the fact that reshaped the design.} There is no single bottleneck. Optimise
either half perfectly and Amdahl caps you \textbf{under 2$\times$}. Every plan that attacked one
half --- more GPUs, more RAM, faster disk --- was aiming at at most half the problem.}

\vspace{0.5em}
{\scriptsize The obvious next move is to produce several tokens per pass, so the 57\% is paid once
for many tokens. That is what batching and speculation do, and it is the next slide.}

\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why batching and speculation both fail --- the deep result}

{\small GLM-5.2 routes \textbf{top 8 of 256} experts per layer. Processing $B$ tokens in one pass
reads the \textbf{union}: $\;256 \times (1 - (1 - 8/256)^{B})$.}

\begin{center}
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{batch} & \textbf{experts} & \textbf{bytes} & \\
\midrule
1 & 8.0 & 1.0$\times$ & one token \\
5 & 37.6 & \textbf{4.7$\times$} & speculation depth 4 \\
8 & 57.4 & \textbf{7.2$\times$} & eight users \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2em}
{\small\textbf{The union grows almost linearly.} The 43\% expert half does not amortise --- only
the 57\% half does. \textbf{And colibr\`i ran this experiment under full residency}, so it is
\meas, not predicted:}

\vspace{0.2em}
\begin{center}
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{sessions} & \textbf{aggregate} & \textbf{per session} & \\
\midrule
1 & 4.84 & \textbf{4.84} & \\
4 & 8.14 & 2.04 & saturated already \\
8 & \textbf{8.30} & \textbf{1.04} & \emph{below} that host's 8.90 single-stream baseline \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2em}
\keybox{\small \textbf{You cannot batch your way to a fast frontier model here.} Multi-user speed
has to come from a \textbf{different model}, not a different setting. Hence three tiers.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two things the last version of this deck got wrong}

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
{\small\textbf{1. ``colibr\`i is single-user.''}\\[0.3em]
It is not. \texttt{SERVE\_BATCH=1} selects a
\textbf{continuous-batching} scheduler with up to 16 KV slots --- \emph{``every active slot
contributes one row per forward.''}

\vspace{0.4em}
The 1.32 / 1.51 / 1.46 tok/s figure I quoted was measured on a host where \textbf{the experts were
not resident}, so extra slots thrashed the cache. It does not transfer.

\vspace{0.4em}
It still does not rescue us --- but for the union reason on the previous slide, not this one.}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
{\small\textbf{2. ``Four GPUs are worth under 2\%.''}\\[0.3em]
That A/B ran on a host pulling \textbf{19.7 GB/s of an available 85} --- 23\% of the machine.
Placement could not matter, because the CPU could not consume faster from either tier.

\vspace{0.4em}
On a host that pulls \textbf{131.9 GB/s} the question reopens: A40 memory is $\sim$696 GB/s, so
experts in VRAM arrive \textbf{$\sim$5$\times$ faster} than from RAM.

\vspace{0.4em}
\textbf{Corrected: open, and a measurement we owe.} Possibly worth 1.6$\times$ on the expert half.}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\vspace{0.6em}
\keybox{Both errors came from carrying somebody else's measurement onto different hardware without
checking what limited it. That is the trap colibr\`i's own experiment log spends a whole section
warning about.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cluster mode --- real, and not yet ready}

{\small colibr\`i can shard experts \emph{across machines}. The coordinator keeps generation,
routing and KV local; expert workers on other nodes run the routed FFNs. \textbf{Only activations
cross the wire} --- about 24\,MB per token.}

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.52\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tikzpicture}[
  font=\scriptsize, >={Stealth[length=2mm]},
  co/.style={draw=blue!55!black, thick, fill=blue!12, align=center, inner sep=4pt,
             minimum height=13mm, minimum width=34mm},
  wk/.style={draw=green!45!black, thick, fill=green!12, align=center, inner sep=3pt,
             minimum height=9mm, minimum width=34mm},

]
\node[co] (c) at (0,0) {\textbf{coordinator}\\routing + KV + attention\\1 GPU};
\node[wk] (w1) at (6.4,1.5)  {worker 1 --- experts $\equiv$ 1};
\node[wk] (w2) at (6.4,0.0)  {worker 2 --- experts $\equiv$ 2};
\node[wk] (w3) at (6.4,-1.5) {worker \emph{N} --- 372/\emph{N}\,GB each};
\draw[<->, thick] (c) -- node[lbl, above, sloped] {activations only} (w1);
\draw[<->, thick] (c) -- (w2);
\draw[<->, thick] (c) -- (w3);
\node[lbl, text=black!55, align=center] at (0,-1.7) {expert $e$, layer $\ell$\\$\to$ worker $(e+\ell)\bmod N$};
\end{tikzpicture}}
\end{center}

\vspace{0em}
{\scriptsize \textbf{The fabric supports it:} \texttt{mlx5\_1} is a \textbf{40 Gb/s Mellanox link,
ACTIVE}. Six nodes reading their own slice in parallel would cut the expert phase from 92\,ms to
about 34.}

\vspace{0.3em}
\keybox{\scriptsize \textbf{Three reasons it is P5, not P1.} The worker loop is
\textbf{synchronous} --- $75 \times N$ sequential round trips per token, and fixing it is an
upstream patch. A worker failure calls \texttt{exit(1)}, so one yielded node kills the service. And
\textbf{activations cross the network in plaintext}, which for PHI-derived hidden states is a new
exposure, not a config detail.}

\end{frame}

                   node[lbl, below] {\texttt{-{}-dependency=afterany}} (g2);
\draw[flow] (g2) -- (g3);
\draw[flow, draw=red!70!black] (k) -- node[lbl, right] {checked first, always} (g2);

\end{tikzpicture}}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2em}
{\small \textbf{Each generation submits its successor the moment it starts}, with a dependency that
fires on \emph{any} termination. A pending job costs nothing, so the queued successor is free
insurance.}

\vspace{0.2em}
\keybox{\scriptsize \textbf{The kill switch is a file, not a signal.} \texttt{fleet down} writes
\texttt{STOP} \emph{before} it cancels anything, so the successor starts, reads it, and exits in two
seconds. It works when the supervisor is wedged and when its node is gone.}

\vspace{0.1em}
{\scriptsize One tier-2 change: \textbf{readiness is a generated token, not a listening socket} ---
the model takes 27 minutes to load, and an ollama-sized timeout fires at 3\% of the way through.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Citizenship --- the ask is now large, and has to be argued}

The standing claim is \textbf{compute306 entire, plus one \texttt{c3} node}. Bigger than either
earlier plan. What makes it defensible:

\begin{itemize}\small
\item \textbf{compute306 is used, not camped on.} It is the only node that can hold the tier-1
      model, and it now serves everybody rather than one person.
\item \textbf{Tier 1 is the rung that gets pulled.} It costs minutes to restart, so it is what the
      fleet yields --- the ladder has more than one rung again.
\item \textbf{Tier 2 costs \meas\ 27 minutes} to reload 372\,GB at 230\,MB/s, so its idle timeout is
      \textbf{three hours}, not thirty minutes. A 27-minute asset released over a lunch break is
      thrashing, and a fleet that thrashes is worse than a fleet that is simply smaller.
\item \textbf{The yield filter is still the load-bearing part.} On the day this was written,
      \textbf{all 27} pending jobs on the cluster were waiting on a start time they had asked for
      --- not on resources. A naive rule would surrender everything, continuously, for nothing.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4em}
\keybox{\small \textbf{Fair-share is no longer a rounding error.} Tier 1 bills $\sim$92 CPUs and
four GPUs; tier 2 bills $\sim$92 CPUs and one. That lands on the PSYCH-ASR and TRD-EHR jobs this
exists to serve, and it must be measured before and during --- not asserted to be fine.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How good will it be --- and how do we know a question is hard?}

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
{\small\textbf{Where open models are closest}
\begin{itemize}\small
\item running commands, reading output
\item iterating on a compile error
\item writing prose to a house style
\item single-step tool use, search
\end{itemize}}
\end{column}

{\small\textbf{Where the gap opens, worst last}
\begin{itemize}\small

\item noticing an absence nobody flagged
\item \textbf{holding a position under pushback}
\end{itemize}}\vspace{-0.4em}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\vspace{0.4em}
\keybox{\scriptsize \textbf{Do not ask the model whether the question is hard.} Confidence
calibration is poor, and worse at int4 --- a model that does not know what it does not know is
precisely the failure this would need to detect. And int4 damage \textbf{concentrates on the
hardest questions}, because per-row scales erode the small logit margins those depend on.}
